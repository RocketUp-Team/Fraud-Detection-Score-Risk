\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:background}

\section{Imbalanced temporal ranking}

Fraud detection is framed here as binary temporal ranking on rare positive cases, with an observed fraud rate of approximately \val{3.5\%}. The model must rank risky transactions highly enough for investigators to review them efficiently, while the evaluation must reflect the fact that future transactions are not available when the model is trained. Chronological splitting along \texttt{TransactionDT} preserves that direction of time and reduces lookahead leakage. The resulting metrics should therefore be interpreted as evidence of performance on a later time window, not as proof that the model will behave identically under every future fraud pattern.

\section{IEEE-CIS dataset structure}

The dataset contains \val{590,540} labelled training transactions together with optional identity records \parencite{ieeecis2019}. Transaction attributes and identity attributes are stored separately, so joining them is a central data-engineering decision. The pipeline preserves the transaction table as the authoritative grain through a LEFT JOIN. This prevents transactions from disappearing merely because no identity record exists, while the resulting missingness is retained as information that can be used by the model and examined during quality analysis.

\section{Dataset provenance and competition context}

IEEE-CIS Fraud Detection was released as a Kaggle research prediction
competition hosted by the IEEE Computational Intelligence Society in 2019.
The competition asked participants to detect fraudulent customer transactions
from structured transaction and identity information, and it attracted a large
community of participants and teams \parencite{ieeecis2019}. The dataset is
useful for this project because it is large enough to require a deliberate
data-processing strategy, contains both numerical and categorical variables,
and represents a fraud setting in which the positive class is rare. It is also
appropriate for studying the boundary between offline model development and
online risk scoring because the transaction and identity information must be
combined before a model can use the full available context.

The public dataset should not be interpreted as a complete representation of a
bank's production data. The fields are anonymised, the original business
definitions of many variables are unavailable, and the recorded competition
period does not cover every geography, merchant population, or fraud pattern.
Consequently, the project uses the data as a reproducible benchmark and
engineering workload. Claims in this report concern the recorded benchmark and
the implemented pipeline; they are not claims about the prevalence of fraud in
the wider financial system.

\section{Raw files, records, and feature groups}

The raw release contains four principal files. The labelled transaction table
contains \val{590,540} rows and 394 columns, including the target
\texttt{isFraud}; the corresponding identity table contains \val{144,233}
rows and 41 columns. The unlabelled test transaction table contains
\val{506,691} rows and 393 columns, while the test identity table contains
\val{141,907} rows and 41 columns. The project inventory records the four files
as approximately \val{1.29~GB} in total. The difference between the number of
transaction rows and identity rows is expected: identity information is
available for only a subset of transactions, which is why a transaction-
authoritative join is required.

The transaction table combines several conceptual groups. Core fields include
\texttt{TransactionID}, \texttt{TransactionDT}, \texttt{TransactionAmt}, and
\texttt{ProductCD}. Card and address fields describe payment-instrument and
location-related patterns, while the \texttt{C}, \texttt{D}, \texttt{M}, and
\texttt{V} families contain anonymised counts, time differences, matching
signals, and engineered behavioural variables. The identity table adds fields
related to device and digital identity, including variables such as
\texttt{DeviceType}, \texttt{DeviceInfo}, and anonymised identity attributes.
Because the variable meanings are intentionally hidden, the pipeline uses
their type, missingness, temporal position, and observed predictive behaviour
without assigning unsupported business interpretations to individual columns.

\section{Target definition and class distribution}

The target variable is \texttt{isFraud}, where the positive value denotes a
fraudulent transaction and the negative value denotes a legitimate transaction.
The local dataset inventory reports \val{20,663} fraud transactions and
\val{569,877} legitimate transactions, producing a fraud rate of
\val{3.499\%} and a legitimate-to-fraud ratio of approximately \val{27.58:1}.
This ratio changes the meaning of every model metric. In particular, a model
that predicts the majority class for almost all rows may appear accurate while
being operationally useless. The project therefore preserves the natural
class distribution in validation and holdout and uses weighting or controlled
sampling only as an explicitly recorded training decision.

\section{Anonymisation, missingness, and reproducibility}

Anonymisation protects the original commercial data but limits the amount of
domain interpretation that can be justified. A variable named \texttt{V123}
cannot be described as a particular customer or device behaviour without an
external data dictionary, so the report refers to feature families and
measured effects rather than inventing semantic meanings. Missing values are
also not automatically treated as noise. The absence of an identity record or
the absence of a device attribute may be correlated with the way a transaction
was collected. The project measures and preserves this missingness, while
training-only imputation prevents information from the validation or holdout
period leaking into the preprocessing state.

The dataset is therefore reproducible at the file, schema, split, and contract
levels. The source inventory identifies the expected files and sizes, the
pipeline records the join and split audits, and the model-ready export freezes
the feature order and preprocessing metadata. Reproducing a numeric result
still requires the same source release, software dependencies, configuration,
and model artefacts; the public availability of the dataset alone does not
guarantee identical results.

\section{Technology boundary}

The architecture deliberately separates \textbf{distributed Spark data preparation from single-node Python estimator training}. Spark is responsible for schema-aware ingestion, joins, temporal transformations, aggregate feature construction, and Parquet handover. After the model-ready contract is produced, Python-based estimators are trained and evaluated against the same frozen feature order. CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost, and Logistic Regression are compared, and TreeSHAP is used to provide local feature attributions for tree-model predictions \parencite{lundberg2017shap}. This boundary satisfies the project requirement to use a big-data processing engine while making the current training-memory limitation explicit.

\section{Why accuracy is insufficient for this dataset}

Accuracy is a poor primary measure when legitimate transactions outnumber
fraudulent transactions by roughly twenty-eight to one. A classifier that labels
almost every row as legitimate can obtain a high accuracy value while detecting
very little fraud. The report therefore gives priority to PR-AUC, which measures
the precision-recall ranking behaviour under the rare-positive setting, and
uses ROC-AUC as a complementary measure of class separation. Precision and
recall at an operating threshold are reported because the review queue has a
finite capacity and because investigators need to understand the trade-off
between missed fraud and additional alerts. The Brier score is also retained
for the calibrated candidate because a usable risk probability should be
interpretable as more than an arbitrary ranking number.

\section{Temporal leakage and operational realism}

Temporal leakage occurs when information from a later period influences a
training feature, preprocessing parameter, threshold, or model-selection
decision. In a fraud system this can happen subtly: an entity aggregate may
include transactions that happened after the row being scored, or an imputer
may be fitted using the distribution of the holdout window. The project reduces
this risk by ordering rows with \texttt{TransactionDT}, fitting learned state
on the earlier training window, selecting candidates on validation, and
reporting final performance on a later holdout. This design is more demanding
than a random split, but it produces evidence that is closer to the actual
operating situation in which the model scores transactions arriving after its
training history.

\section{Interpretability as a review requirement}

The system treats explainability as an operational requirement rather than a
decorative visual. A fraud analyst needs to know which input characteristics
contributed to a high score, especially when a legitimate customer may be
temporarily blocked or escalated. TreeSHAP provides local attributions for
tree-based models, allowing the interface to display the strongest positive
and negative contributions for an individual transaction. These attributions
explain the model's association with the observed feature vector; they do not
prove that a feature caused the transaction to be fraudulent. That distinction
is preserved in the report and in the application wording.
